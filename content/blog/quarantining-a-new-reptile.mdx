Bringing a new reptile home is exciting, and the temptation is to put it straight into its beautiful permanent enclosure next to your other animals. Resist it. A short quarantine period — keeping the new arrival separate while you watch its health — is one of the most important habits in reptile keeping, and it protects both the new animal and every reptile you already own.

## Why quarantine matters

A reptile can look perfectly healthy and still carry parasites or an infection. Mites, internal parasites, respiratory infections and mouth rot often show no obvious signs for days or weeks, especially in an animal stressed by transport and a new home. If that animal goes straight into a shared room or enclosure, mites spread fast, and an infection can pass to your established animals before you notice anything wrong.

Quarantine is not about distrusting the breeder or shop you bought from. Even well-cared-for animals can pick up a problem during shipping, at a show, or from a shared holding room. Treating every new arrival the same way, every time, removes the guesswork.

## How long should quarantine last?

A good rule of thumb is 60 to 90 days — long enough for most common problems to show themselves: mites usually appear within a few weeks, and respiratory infections tend to surface once the stress of the move catches up with the animal. Thirty days is the absolute minimum, and if anything looks off, the clock resets.

## Setting up a simple quarantine enclosure

Quarantine housing should be simple, not fancy. A plain setup makes it much easier to spot droppings, mites, and changes in behaviour. A [quarantine tub with a locking vented lid](/shop/enclosures/reptile-quarantine-tub) is ideal: secure, easy to clean, and inexpensive. Line the bottom with plain white paper towel, which makes mites, unusual droppings and regurgitated food easy to see. Add one hide, a water bowl, and the correct heating for the species — a [digital thermometer and hygrometer](/shop/care-accessories/digital-thermometer-hygrometer) helps you confirm the temperatures are right, since a cold reptile cannot digest food or fight off infection properly.

Keep the quarantine enclosure in a separate room from your other reptiles if you can. If that is not possible, keep it as far away as you can, and always handle and feed your established animals first, then the quarantined one. Wash your hands thoroughly between animals, and do not share tongs, bowls, hides or decor between enclosures. Clean quarantine equipment last, so nothing from it touches your main collection.

## What to watch for every day

Spend a few minutes each day observing your new reptile. Our guide to the [signs of a healthy reptile](/blog/signs-of-a-healthy-reptile) covers the baseline in detail, but during quarantine, pay special attention to these:

- **Eating and drinking.** Most reptiles will eat within a week or two of settling in. Refusing food for longer than that, or eating and then regurgitating, is worth investigating.
- **Breathing.** Listen for wheezing, clicking or bubbling, and watch for mucus around the mouth or nostrils, or a head held raised at an odd angle. These can signal a respiratory infection.
- **Skin and shedding.** Check for tiny black or red dots moving on the skin or in the water bowl — snake mites are about the size of a pinhead. Watch for patchy, stuck shed, which can point to a humidity problem or an underlying health issue.
- **Droppings.** Note the colour and consistency. Runny, foul-smelling or bloody droppings are a reason to contact a reptile vet, and a faecal sample is the easiest way for a vet to check for internal parasites.
- **Weight.** Weigh the animal weekly on a [digital gram scale](/shop/care-accessories/digital-gram-scale) and write the numbers down. Steady weight is reassuring; a steady decline is an early warning sign that something is wrong.

## When to call a reptile vet

Do not wait out serious symptoms. Book a vet visit if you see mites, discharge from the eyes, nose or mouth, laboured breathing, swelling, a wound that is not healing, or weight loss that continues for more than a couple of weeks. Many vets also recommend a routine check-up and faecal test for any new arrival, which can catch internal parasites before they become a problem. Bring a fresh dropping in a sealed bag or container if the vet asks for one.

## Ending quarantine the right way

Quarantine ends when the animal has been healthy for the full period: eating regularly, shedding cleanly, gaining or holding weight, and showing no sign of mites or illness. Before the move, clean and disinfect the permanent enclosure and all its decor with a [reptile-safe cleaner](/shop/care-accessories/reptile-safe-cleaner) — our [enclosure cleaning routine](/blog/cleaning-and-disinfecting-your-reptiles-enclosure) walks through the steps. Then move the animal in and keep watching it for another week or two, just as you would any new arrival settling into a new space.

A quarantine period costs little: a tub, some paper towel and a few minutes of observation a day. What it buys is peace of mind — the confidence that your new reptile is healthy, and that your existing animals stay that way.
