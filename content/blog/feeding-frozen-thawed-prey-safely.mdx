Most pet snakes eat frozen-thawed rodents, and for good reason. A frozen mouse or rat cannot bite or injure your snake the way live prey can, and frozen rodents are easy to store and come in sizes for every snake. The catch is that a frozen rodent is only good food if it is thawed and warmed properly. Get the method wrong and your snake may refuse the meal, or worse, regurgitate it.

## Choose the right size first

The prey item should be about as wide as the widest part of your snake's body, or roughly 10 to 15 percent of the snake's body weight. Too small and the snake stays hungry; too large and it may struggle to swallow, or bring the meal back up hours later.

Hatchlings and small species start on pinky mice. Young ball pythons and corn snakes move up through fuzzy and hopper mice, then adult mice, and larger species graduate to rats. Our [frozen adult mice](/shop/food-supplements/frozen-mice-adult-10) suit most juvenile and adult colubrids, while [frozen small rats](/shop/food-supplements/frozen-rats-small-5) are the right step up for snakes that have outgrown mice. When in doubt, go one size smaller — a slightly small meal is always safer than one that is too big.

## Thaw slowly, never in the microwave

Take the rodent out of the freezer and thaw it in the fridge for several hours, or overnight for larger rats. If you are short on time, seal it in a zip-lock bag and place it in a bowl of cool water, changing the water every 15 minutes until it is fully thawed.

Never thaw a rodent in the microwave. Microwaves heat unevenly, so one part can be scalding hot while the centre is still frozen. A hot spot can burn your snake's mouth, and a partly frozen core can cause regurgitation. Never use boiling water for the same reason.

## Warm it to body temperature

Snakes hunt by heat as much as by sight. A thawed rodent straight from the fridge is cold and uninteresting to a snake that expects a warm animal. Warm the rodent in a bowl of warm — not hot — water for 10 to 15 minutes, until it feels about the same temperature as your hand, around 37°C (98°F), then dry it with a paper towel so it does not pick up substrate.

## Offer with tongs, not fingers

Always offer prey with long feeding tongs, never with your fingers. A snake striking at food cannot tell a warm hand from a warm mouse, and a feeding bite is painful even from a small snake. [Stainless steel feeding tongs](/shop/care-accessories/feeding-tongs-12in) keep your hand a safe distance away and let you wiggle the prey to mimic movement, which is often what triggers the strike.

Hold the rodent by the scruff or tail and dangle it a few centimetres in front of the snake's head, moving it slowly from side to side. Most snakes strike within a minute or two. If yours does not, leave the rodent on a clean surface in the enclosure and check back in an hour — some snakes prefer to eat in private and in the dark. Never leave an uneaten rodent in the enclosure overnight. Throw it away and try again in a few days.

## After the meal: hands off

Once your snake has swallowed, leave it completely alone for at least 48 hours. Handling or moving the enclosure too soon can cause regurgitation, which is stressful and hard on the snake's digestive system. Make sure the warm end of the enclosure is at the right temperature, because snakes need belly heat to digest. Our [reptile heating guide](/blog/reptile-heating-guide) explains how to set that up if you are unsure.

If your snake does regurgitate, do not feed it again straight away. Wait at least 10 to 14 days, then offer a smaller prey item than usual. Repeated regurgitation is a sign something is wrong — wrong temperatures, stress, or an illness — and it is time to see a reptile vet.

## How often should you feed?

Feeding frequency depends on the species and the snake's age. Hatchlings and juveniles usually eat every 5 to 7 days, because they are growing fast. Adults slow down: most adult ball pythons and corn snakes do well on one appropriately sized meal every 7 to 14 days, and large constrictors may eat only every 2 to 3 weeks. Overfeeding is a real problem in captivity — an overweight snake lives a shorter, less healthy life. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can I refreeze a thawed rodent my snake refused?

No. Once a rodent has been thawed and warmed, bacteria multiply quickly, and refreezing does not make it safe again. Discard any uneaten prey.

### My snake won't eat frozen-thawed prey. What should I do?

First check the basics: correct temperatures, a proper hide, and no recent stress such as a move or a shed. Try warming the prey a little more, offering it at night, or scenting it with used mouse bedding. Ball pythons are famous for fasting, especially in winter — a healthy adult refusing food for a few weeks is usually not an emergency, but see a vet if the snake starts losing weight.

### How long does it take to thaw a frozen rodent?

Several hours in the fridge, or overnight for larger rats. If you are short on time, seal the rodent in a zip-lock bag and place it in a bowl of cool water, changing the water every 15 minutes until it is fully thawed. Never use a microwave or boiling water — uneven heating can burn your snake's mouth or leave a frozen core that causes regurgitation.
