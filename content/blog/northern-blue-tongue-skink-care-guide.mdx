The [northern blue-tongued skink](/shop/live-reptiles/northern-blue-tongued-skink) is a hefty, ground-dwelling lizard with broad bands, warm orange-gold flanks and the cobalt-blue tongue that gives the group its name. That tongue is a defence display, flashed to startle a predator, not a sign of a bad temper. Settled animals are usually calm and curious.

<Note>
Every animal is an individual. Use this guide as a starting point and consult a qualified reptile veterinarian about any health concern.
</Note>

## Meet the northern blue-tongued skink

Northern blue-tongued skinks (*Tiliqua scincoides intermedia*) come from northern Australia, where they shelter in leaf litter, under logs and in burrows, coming out during the day to forage. They are among the largest blue-tongued skinks: adults typically reach about 18–24 inches (45–60 cm), and with good care they can live 15–20 years or more.

They are omnivores, taking insects and other invertebrates alongside plant material, and they give birth to live young rather than laying eggs. Their short legs and long bodies make them poor climbers, so design the enclosure for walking, digging and basking at ground level.

## Housing: long, secure and easy to clean

Floor space matters most. A 4 × 2 ft enclosure is a sensible minimum for one adult, such as our [4 × 2 × 2 ft PVC enclosure](/shop/enclosures/pvc-enclosure-4x2x2), and larger is better if you have the room. Front-opening doors make cleaning and feeding easier and are less stressful than reaching in from above.

Use a substrate the skink can dig in, deep enough to hold some moisture without staying wet. Provide a hide at each end, a flat basking surface and a heavy water dish that cannot be tipped over. Cork bark and leaf litter give the animal places to explore. A [blue-tongue skink starter kit](/shop/enclosures/blue-tongue-skink-starter-kit) can help assemble the basics, but check that the finished setup meets the temperatures below.

## Heat, UVB and humidity

Aim for a basking surface of about 95–105°F (35–41°C) with a cooler side around 75–80°F (24–27°C), so the animal can warm up, digest food and move away to cool down. Control the heat source with a thermostat and measure the basking surface directly. Our [reptile heating guide](/blog/reptile-heating-guide) explains gradients and thermostats in detail.

Provide UVB lighting during the day, mounted at the distance the manufacturer recommends for the tube you choose, such as a [T5 HO 6% UVB tube](/shop/heating-lighting/t5-ho-uvb-6-22in). UVB helps the skink produce vitamin D3 and use dietary calcium properly. Our [UVB lighting guide](/blog/uvb-lighting-explained) covers tube choice, placement and when to replace a tube.

Keep humidity moderate, roughly 40–60%, with fresh water always available. A slightly damper hide can help during shedding, but the enclosure should not stay soggy. Check humidity with a hygrometer placed where the animal lives.

## Diet: a varied omnivore mix

In captivity, offer a varied mix built around leafy greens and vegetables, lean protein and only a little fruit. Suitable foods include chopped leafy greens, squash and other safe vegetables, feeder insects such as dubia roaches, and occasional lean protein sources in small amounts. Variety over the week matters more than any single item.

A prepared food such as our [blue-tongue skink diet](/shop/food-supplements/blue-tongue-skink-diet) can form part of that rotation. Dust food lightly with calcium regularly; our [supplements guide](/blog/calcium-and-vitamin-supplements-for-reptiles) explains calcium and multivitamin use. Feed juveniles more often than adults, remove uneaten food before it spoils, and adjust portions to body condition. A healthy adult looks sturdy without folds of fat around the legs and tail base.

## Handling and daily care

Handle gently, support the whole body and keep sessions short at first. Let the animal walk through your hands rather than gripping it, and return it if it becomes restless. Wash your hands before and after handling.

Spot-clean daily, refresh the water, and look the skink over as you do: clear eyes, a clean vent, intact toes and steady weight are reassuring. Weigh it periodically and keep brief notes, because slow weight change is easy to miss.

Get the basics right — a long enclosure, a hot basking spot with a cooler retreat, daytime UVB, moderate humidity and a varied omnivore diet — and a northern blue-tongued skink is a personable, rewarding lizard that will reward years of steady care.

## Frequently asked questions

### How big do northern blue-tongued skinks get?

Adults typically reach about 18–24 inches (45–60 cm). They are ground dwellers, so a long enclosure with plenty of floor space suits them better than a tall one.

### What should a northern blue-tongued skink eat?

A varied omnivore diet of leafy greens and vegetables, lean protein and feeder insects, with only a little fruit. Dust food lightly with calcium as part of a regular supplement routine, and adjust portions to body condition.

### Do blue-tongued skinks need UVB light?

Yes. Daytime UVB, mounted at the manufacturer's recommended distance and replaced on schedule, helps the skink produce vitamin D3 and use dietary calcium properly.

### How warm should the enclosure be?

Provide a basking surface of about 95–105°F (35–41°C) and a cooler side around 75–80°F (24–27°C). Always use a thermostat and check the basking surface temperature directly.
