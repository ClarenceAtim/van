The [western hognose snake](/shop/live-reptiles/western-hognose-snake) is famous for two things: an upturned snout built for digging, and a theatrical defence display. Startled hognoses hiss, flatten their necks like a tiny cobra and, if that fails, roll over and play dead. Day to day, though, they are curious, active little snakes that spend much of their time burrowing and exploring.

<Note>
Every animal is an individual. Use this guide as a starting point and consult a qualified reptile veterinarian about any health concern. Check local rules before buying, as some places restrict hognose snakes.
</Note>

## Meet the western hognose

Western hognoses (*Heterodon nasicus*) come from the dry prairies and sandy soils of central North America, where they dig for toads and shelter underground. Adults stay small: males usually reach about 15–24 inches (38–61 cm) and females about 2–3 feet (61–91 cm). With good care they commonly live 15–20 years.

Hognoses are rear-fanged and produce a mild venom used to subdue prey. Bites to people are uncommon and usually cause only local swelling or irritation, but they are not impossible, so handle any snake with respect and never let one chew on a finger. Their bluffing displays look dramatic, yet a settled captive animal typically calms down quickly once it learns you are not a threat.

## Housing: secure, roomy and deep

Floor space and burrowing depth matter more than height. A 36 × 18 × 18 inch front-opening terrarium, such as our [36 × 18 × 18 in glass terrarium](/shop/enclosures/glass-terrarium-36x18x18), suits most adults, while juveniles do well starting in a smaller secure enclosure so they feel safe and find food easily. Whatever you choose, the lid or doors must lock: hognoses push at gaps and are strong for their size.

Give at least 4 inches of loose, dry substrate the snake can tunnel through, such as [aspen snake bedding](/shop/substrate-decor/aspen-snake-bedding-24qt) or a suitable soil-based mix. Add a hide at the warm end and one at the cool end, a sturdy water dish, and a few pieces of cork bark or wood for cover. A hognose with deep substrate and places to hide is a confident hognose.

## Heat, light and humidity

Provide a warm basking area of about 90–95°F (32–35°C) and a cooler side around 75–80°F (24–27°C), so the snake can choose its temperature. Always control the heat source with a thermostat and check surface temperatures with a thermometer rather than guessing. Our [reptile heating guide](/blog/reptile-heating-guide) explains gradients and thermostats in detail.

Keep humidity moderate, roughly 30–50%, with a slight rise helping during shedding. Provide fresh water at all times. Low-level UVB lighting for a normal daytime cycle can support natural behaviour and overall health, mounted at the distance the manufacturer recommends; our [UVB lighting guide](/blog/uvb-lighting-explained) covers placement and replacement.

## Feeding: frozen-thawed mice

Captive western hognoses are usually fed frozen-thawed mice, which avoids the injury risk of live prey. Choose a mouse about as wide as, or slightly wider than, the snake's widest point. Juveniles typically eat a pinky mouse every 5–7 days, while adults usually eat every 7–14 days depending on prey size and body condition.

Thaw prey fully, warm it gently, and offer it with tongs so the snake does not learn to strike at hands. Our guide to [feeding frozen-thawed prey safely](/blog/feeding-frozen-thawed-prey-safely) covers thawing, warming and what to do if a snake refuses. Some babies are picky at first and may need scenting or a quieter feeding routine, so buy from a breeder who can confirm the snake is already feeding reliably. Never handle for a day or two after a meal.

## Handling and daily care

Handle briefly and gently, supporting the body and letting the snake move through your hands. Short, calm sessions build trust; long sessions and grabbing from above do the opposite. Wash your hands before and after handling, especially after touching rodents.

Spot-clean waste promptly, refresh water regularly, and check the snake as you do: clear eyes, a clean vent, smooth skin and a steady weight are reassuring signs. Weigh it periodically and keep notes.

Get the basics right — a secure enclosure, deep substrate, a proper heat gradient, moderate humidity and appropriately sized frozen-thawed mice — and a western hognose is a characterful, manageable snake that rewards keepers who enjoy watching a small animal dig, explore and bluff its way through the day.

## Frequently asked questions

### How big do western hognose snakes get?

Males usually reach about 15–24 inches (38–61 cm) and females about 2–3 feet (61–91 cm). Females are noticeably larger and heavier-bodied than males.

### Are western hognose snakes venomous?

They are rear-fanged with a mild venom for subduing prey. Bites are uncommon and usually cause only local swelling or irritation, but handle with respect, use tongs at feeding time, and check local rules before buying.

### What should a western hognose eat?

Frozen-thawed mice sized about as wide as the snake's widest point. Juveniles typically eat every 5–7 days and adults every 7–14 days, adjusted for prey size and body condition.

### Do western hognoses need deep substrate?

Yes. They are natural burrowers, so provide at least 4 inches of loose, dry substrate they can tunnel through, plus hides at both the warm and cool ends of the enclosure.
