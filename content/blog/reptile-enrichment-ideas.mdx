In the wild, a reptile's day is full of decisions: where to bask, what to climb, where to hide, what to hunt. In a bare glass box, those decisions disappear — and a bored reptile often becomes a stressed, inactive one. Enrichment is simply giving your pet things to do: places to climb, things to explore, food to work for. It does not need to be expensive or complicated.

## Why enrichment matters

A reptile that has nothing to do tends to sit in one spot all day. Over time that can mean weaker muscles, weight gain, and stress behaviours such as glass surfing, refusing food, or hiding constantly. Our guide to the [signs of a healthy reptile](/blog/signs-of-a-healthy-reptile) covers what normal behaviour looks like; enrichment helps keep that behaviour normal.

## Climbing and perching

Many reptiles climb, even species people think of as ground-dwellers. Bearded dragons, crested geckos, corn snakes and leopard geckos all use vertical space when it is offered safely. Sturdy branches give them a route up to a basking spot and a vantage point to watch the room from. A set of [grapevine branches](/shop/substrate-decor/grapevine-branches-3-pack) can be wedged securely between the enclosure walls or leaned against a hide so they cannot roll or fall. A [basking rock ledge](/shop/substrate-decor/basking-rock-ledge) adds a second level and a warm platform under the heat lamp.

Make sure anything climbable is stable: a branch that tips over can injure a small animal. For heavy-bodied snakes, keep branches low and thick. For arboreal species, offer several routes at different heights so the animal can choose.

## Hides, cover and privacy

A reptile that feels exposed will not explore. Hides are the foundation of enrichment because they make the rest of the enclosure feel safe enough to use. Provide at least two: one on the warm side and one on the cool side, so the animal never has to choose between feeling safe and being the right temperature. A [half log hide](/shop/substrate-decor/half-log-hide-large) works well for many species and doubles as a climbing surface.

Plants add cover too. Live or artificial foliage breaks up sightlines and gives shy animals a corridor to move through. An [artificial pothos vine](/shop/substrate-decor/artificial-pothos-vine) draped along a wall creates instant cover with no watering or lighting needs. If you use live plants, choose species that are safe for reptiles and sturdy enough to survive being walked on — our guide to [setting up a bioactive terrarium](/blog/how-to-set-up-a-bioactive-terrarium) covers plant choices in detail.

## Make feeding interesting

Food is the easiest enrichment of all. Instead of dropping every meal in the same spot, vary it. For insect-eating lizards, let a few feeders roam the enclosure so the animal has to hunt them down, and offer insects from tongs at different heights so it has to reach or climb. For snakes, present the prey in a different corner each time. Scatter-feeding — spreading food around the enclosure rather than piling it in one bowl — works well for tortoises and omnivorous lizards.

Always supervise live feeding, and never leave live insects in the enclosure overnight: crickets can bite a sleeping reptile. Remove anything uneaten after feeding time.

## Rearrange, rotate, refresh

Reptiles notice change. Moving a hide to the other side of the enclosure, swapping in a new branch, or adding a different texture — a flat stone, a piece of cork bark, a new plant — turns a familiar space into somewhere worth exploring again. You do not need new items every week; rotating the ones you already have is enough.

Watch how your reptile responds. If it starts using a new branch within a day, that item is a hit. If it avoids an area completely, something about it feels wrong — too exposed, too hot, or too unstable — and it is worth adjusting.

## What to avoid

Enrichment should be safe first. Skip anything with sharp edges, small loose parts that could be swallowed, or materials that splinter. Avoid painted or treated wood unless it is sold as reptile-safe, and never use pine or cedar, whose aromatic oils can irritate a reptile's respiratory system. Anything you bring in from outside — a branch from the garden, a rock from a walk — should be scrubbed and baked or boiled before it goes into the enclosure.

Keep it simple, change one thing at a time, and let your reptile show you what it likes. A few branches, a good hide, some cover and a feeding routine with a bit of variety will do more for your pet's wellbeing than any single gadget — and watching a lizard climb a branch it ignored yesterday is half the fun.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do reptiles really get bored?

Reptiles do not get bored the way people do, but they do show stress and inactivity in a bare enclosure — sitting in one spot, refusing food, or pacing the glass. Enrichment gives them choices and activity, which supports natural behaviour and better health.

### How often should I change my reptile's enrichment?

Small changes every one to two weeks are enough: move a hide, swap a branch, add a new texture. Avoid redecorating the whole enclosure at once, which can stress the animal.

### Is enrichment safe for hatchlings and juveniles?

Yes, with size in mind. Young reptiles need smaller hides, lower climbing heights and nothing they can get stuck under or behind. Keep items stable and sized to the animal.
