Handling your reptile is one of the best parts of keeping one — it is how you check its health and build trust. But reptiles are not dogs. They do not seek out affection, and a handling session that feels fun to you can feel like a predator attack to them. The goal is calm, confident handling that your reptile learns to tolerate and eventually barely notices.

## Let your reptile settle in first

The most common beginner mistake is handling a new reptile on day one. A new arrival needs time to learn that its enclosure is safe before it can learn that you are safe. Leave a new reptile completely alone for at least a week — two weeks is better for shy species — apart from feeding and changing water. Our guide to [preparing your home for a new reptile](/blog/preparing-your-home-for-a-new-reptile) covers the first-week setup in detail.

Use the time to observe instead: when it basks, eats, explores and hides. That baseline helps you tell normal caution from real stress once handling starts.

## Learn to read body language first

A reptile tells you how it feels if you know what to look for. A relaxed snake moves slowly, tongue-flicking as it explores; a relaxed lizard sits calmly, body loose. Those are animals you can handle.

Stop and try another day if you see stress signals: a snake coiled tight with its head pulled back, hissing, striking or musking; a lizard puffing up, gaping, darkening in colour, whipping its tail or trying to bolt. These are not attitude problems — they are fear. Forcing the issue teaches the animal that handling is something to fear, and the next session gets harder. Our [signs of a healthy reptile](/blog/signs-of-a-healthy-reptile) guide explains what normal, alert behaviour looks like across species.

<Note>
Wash your hands before and after every handling session. Before, so your hands do not smell of food or another animal; after, because reptiles can carry Salmonella. Warm water and soap are enough.
</Note>

## Picking up a snake

Approach a snake from the side, at its own level, never swooping down from above — that is exactly how a hawk attacks, and the snake knows it. Scoop the middle of the body up with one hand and support it with the other, letting the snake's weight rest across your hands rather than dangling from one grip. Let it move through your hands; a snake that can crawl feels safer than one pinned in place.

For larger or defensive snakes, a [collapsible snake hook](/shop/care-accessories/collapsible-snake-hook) helps. Use it to lift the front of the body out of the enclosure, then take over with your hands once the snake is out and calm. The hook keeps your hands away from the strike zone during the riskiest moment — the pickup. Never lift a snake by its tail or by the head alone.

## Handling lizards and geckos

Lizards need the same slow approach, with one extra rule: never grab a lizard by the tail. Many species can drop their tails to escape, and a regrown tail never looks the same. Scoop from underneath instead, supporting the belly and all four feet so the animal does not feel like it is falling.

Small, fast species need more care. A crested gecko may jump without warning, so handle it sitting down over a soft surface, or inside the enclosure with the doors closed behind you. Keep sessions short for small lizards — five minutes is plenty — and end by placing the animal back gently. Use [feeding tongs](/shop/care-accessories/feeding-tongs-12in) to offer food instead of your fingers, so the animal never confuses a hand with a meal.

## Short sessions, built up slowly

Start with five to ten minutes, two or three times a week, and build up from there. Short, calm, regular sessions teach a reptile that handling is a normal, boring part of life. Long, rare sessions do the opposite.

Don't handle for 48 hours after a meal, or during a shed cycle, when the animal can barely see and is more defensive than usual. A cold reptile is a sluggish, stressed one, so check the temperature first — a [digital thermometer and hygrometer](/shop/care-accessories/digital-thermometer-hygrometer) tells you in seconds whether the warm side is where it should be.

## Mistakes that undo your progress

- **Grabbing from above.** Always approach at the animal's level.
- **Squeezing or restraining.** A firm grip that stops all movement feels like being caught. Support, don't restrain.
- **Handling a sick or shedding animal.** Leave it alone until it is eating and shedding normally again.
- **Letting children handle unsupervised.** Children squeeze and drop animals easily — an adult should always be in control.
- **Rushing it.** A reptile that takes a month to relax in your hands is normal, not a failure. Some individuals stay shy for life, and that is fine too.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long until my reptile is tame?

Most captive-bred snakes and lizards settle within a few weeks of short, calm sessions; some take months. Nervous species may never enjoy handling, but they can still learn to tolerate it.

### How often should I handle my reptile?

Two or three short sessions a week suits most species. Daily handling is fine for calm, settled animals — but give rest days around feeding and shedding.

### My reptile bit me. What did I do wrong?

Probably nothing serious. Most bites happen because the animal was startled, smelled food on your hands, or felt cornered. Stay calm, don't yank your hand away, and let it release on its own. Wash the bite, and next time move slower and wash your hands first.
