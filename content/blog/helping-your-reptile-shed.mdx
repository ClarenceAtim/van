Every reptile sheds its skin. Snakes do it in one long piece, lizards and geckos in patches and flakes. A clean shed is one of the best signs your husbandry is right. A ragged, stuck shed is usually the first warning that something in the enclosure needs fixing — most often the humidity.

## What a normal shed looks like

A shed does not happen suddenly. In the days before it, your reptile's colours go dull and its skin looks dry or greyish. Snakes go through a "blue" phase: their eyes turn milky blue for a few days as fluid builds up between the old and new skin layers, then clear again just before the shed. Appetite often drops, and the animal may hide more and bask less.

Then the old skin comes off. A snake rubs its nose against a rough surface until the skin splits, then crawls out of it, turning it inside out like a sock — ideally in one complete piece, eye caps included. Lizards shed in sections over a day or two and often eat the shed skin. Young, fast-growing animals shed every four to six weeks; adults shed less often.

<Note>
Never pull shed skin off your reptile. Skin that is still attached is still attached to living tissue underneath, and pulling it can tear the new skin and cause infection. If a piece will not come off on its own, it needs moisture and patience — not force.
</Note>

## Humidity is the key factor

Almost every bad shed comes back to humidity that is too low for the species. Dry air dries the old skin out before it can separate cleanly, so it tears and sticks. Measure first, then adjust: check the humidity range in your species' care guide, and verify the enclosure with a [digital thermometer and hygrometer](/shop/care-accessories/digital-thermometer-hygrometer) rather than guessing. Our [reptile humidity guide](/blog/reptile-humidity-guide) explains how to raise or lower humidity for your setup.

Practical ways to keep humidity in range during a shed cycle:

- **Mist the enclosure** lightly in the morning with a [pressure sprayer](/shop/care-accessories/pressure-sprayer-2l), especially around the hide and substrate.
- **Offer a humid hide.** A [humid hide cave](/shop/substrate-decor/humid-hide-cave) filled with damp sphagnum moss gives your reptile a moist retreat while the old skin loosens. Keep the moss damp, not soaking, and replace it regularly.
- **Keep the water dish full.** A clean dish adds moisture to the air and gives the animal somewhere to soak.
- **Do not overdo it.** Humidity far above the species' range for weeks on end invites respiratory infections and scale rot, so aim for the species' normal range — just the upper end of it while shedding.

## How to help a shedding reptile

Most of the time the best help is to leave the animal alone and make sure the enclosure is right. Handling a reptile mid-shed stresses it and can damage the new skin, and snakes in their blue phase can barely see, so they are more defensive than usual.

Make sure the enclosure has something rough to rub against: a piece of cork bark, a rough stone or a sturdy branch. Smooth glass and plastic give a snake nothing to catch the old skin on. If a few small pieces remain stuck after the main shed, a lukewarm soak can help — shallow water, about belly height, for 15 to 20 minutes, supervised the whole time. Afterwards, let the animal crawl through a damp towel in your hands; the gentle friction often takes the loosened pieces off.

## Stuck shed: when to worry

A few small stuck patches after an otherwise complete shed are common and usually resolve with a humid hide and a soak. Some spots matter more than others:

- **Toes and tail tips.** A tight ring of stuck shed can cut off circulation, and toes or tail tips can be lost if it is left for weeks. Check these after every shed.
- **Eyes.** Retained eye caps in snakes look like a cloudy, wrinkled cap over the eye after the rest of the shed is done. Do not try to remove these yourself.
- **Repeated bad sheds.** If every shed is patchy despite correct humidity, something else is going on — dehydration, mites, a skin infection, low temperatures or a vitamin deficiency.

See a reptile vet if shed is stuck around toes, tail or eyes, if the skin underneath looks red, raw or swollen, or if bad sheds keep happening after you have fixed the humidity. A vet can remove retained shed safely and check for the underlying cause.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should my reptile shed?

It depends on age and species. Juveniles growing fast may shed every four to six weeks, while adults shed a few times a year. What matters is whether each shed comes off cleanly.

### My snake's eyes went blue. Is it sick?

No — milky blue eyes are a normal stage of the shed cycle. The eyes clear again a few days before the shed. If the eyes stay cloudy after the shed is complete, or the snake seems unwell in other ways, see a vet.

### Should I feed my reptile while it is shedding?

Many reptiles refuse food while shedding, and that is normal. Offer on the usual schedule and remove uneaten prey; appetite usually returns within a day or two after the shed.
