A reptile spends its whole life inside its enclosure, eating, shedding and going to the bathroom in the same small space. Waste, spilled water and leftover food quickly grow bacteria, and a dirty enclosure is one of the fastest ways to make a healthy reptile sick. The good news: a few minutes a day, a short weekly clean, and a proper deep clean once a month is enough for most setups.

## Why cleaning matters so much

Reptiles are good at hiding illness, so by the time a problem is visible it is often advanced. Bacteria from old droppings and damp substrate are a common cause of skin infections, mouth rot and respiratory problems. Dirty water bowls are another frequent culprit: a bowl that looks fine can carry a heavy bacterial load within a day or two. Reptiles can also carry Salmonella without showing symptoms, and it spreads through droppings and contaminated surfaces, so wash your hands with soap after every cleaning session and never wash enclosure items in a sink you use for food preparation.

## Daily: spot-clean in under five minutes

Spot-cleaning is the habit that makes everything else easy. Once a day, remove any droppings and urates (the white, chalky part of reptile waste), pick out uneaten food, and wipe up spilled water. Empty, rinse and refill the water bowl every day. A bowl with a slimy film needs a scrub, not just a refill.

If you keep a species that eats live insects, remove any uneaten feeders at the end of the day too. Loose crickets left overnight will chew on a sleeping reptile.

## Weekly: a proper wipe-down

Once a week, take out the water bowl, food dish and any hides or decor that are easy to move, and wash them with hot water and a reptile-safe cleaner. Scrub the walls where waste or water marks have built up, and replace soiled or damp substrate. Our [substrate guide](/blog/choosing-the-right-substrate-for-your-reptile) explains which beddings hold up best between changes. While you are at it, check for mould under the water bowl, damp patches in the substrate, and any sharp edges or damage on hides and decor.

## Monthly: the full deep clean

Once a month, give the whole enclosure a deep clean. Move your reptile to a secure temporary container first — a ventilated tub or a [snake bag inside a rigid box](/shop/care-accessories/snake-bags-3-pack) — and never leave it unsupervised.

Remove everything: substrate, hides, branches, dishes and decor. Throw away all the old substrate. Wash the empty enclosure with hot water and a [reptile-safe enclosure cleaner](/shop/care-accessories/reptile-safe-cleaner), scrubbing every corner, then rinse thoroughly and let it dry completely before putting fresh substrate in. Decor can be scrubbed the same way; wood and porous items may need a longer soak and a full air-dry, since disinfectant trapped in pores can irritate your animal later. While everything is out, wipe dust off bulbs and fixtures and confirm the thermostat is still reading correctly.

## Which cleaners are safe — and which are not

Never use household cleaners, bleach, or anything scented inside a reptile enclosure. Bleach fumes and residues are toxic to reptiles, and scented products leave chemicals on surfaces your animal will lick, climb on and sleep against. Even "natural" cleaners with essential oils are not safe.

Use only products made for reptile enclosures, or a dilute veterinary disinfectant such as chlorhexidine or F10, used exactly as the label directs. Rinse well and let surfaces dry fully before your reptile goes back in — if you can still smell the cleaner, it is not ready.

## Bioactive enclosures are different

If you run a bioactive setup with live plants, springtails and isopods doing the clean-up work, do not strip it monthly. The clean-up crew needs time to establish, and a full deep clean destroys the colony. Instead, spot-clean visible waste, remove uneaten food, clean the water bowl as usual, and refresh part of the substrate only when the soil is breaking down or smelling off. Our guide to [setting up a bioactive terrarium](/blog/how-to-set-up-a-bioactive-terrarium) covers how the system works.

## A routine you can actually keep

Tie the daily spot-clean to something you already do — feeding time works well — and put the weekly and monthly cleans on the same day each week and month so you do not have to remember. Five minutes a day and one longer session a month is all it takes, and a clean enclosure is the single easiest thing you can do for your reptile's health.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I change the substrate completely?

For most non-bioactive setups, a full change once a month is a good baseline, with soiled patches replaced as you spot-clean. Messy species or very humid enclosures may need it more often.

### Can I use vinegar to clean the enclosure?

White vinegar is sometimes suggested for removing water stains on glass, but it is not a disinfectant, and any residue can irritate reptiles. A purpose-made reptile cleaner is safer and more effective. If you do use vinegar on glass, rinse thoroughly and let it dry completely before your animal returns.

### My enclosure smells even after cleaning. What is wrong?

A lingering smell usually means waste has soaked into porous decor, the substrate is staying damp, or ventilation is blocked. Replace porous wood and cork that will not come clean, and check the substrate is not waterlogged.
