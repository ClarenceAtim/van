Substrate is the bedding that lines the floor of your reptile's enclosure, and it does far more than make the tank look natural. It holds or releases moisture, gives your animal something to dig in, cushions falls and affects how easy the enclosure is to keep clean. Choose wrong and you can end up fighting humidity problems, or worse, a vet visit. Choose right and the enclosure almost takes care of itself.

The rule is simple: match the substrate to the animal's natural habitat, not to what looks good on a shelf.

## Start with your species, not the bag

A tropical species that comes from a humid rainforest needs a substrate that holds moisture — coconut fibre, cypress mulch or a soil mix. An arid species from a desert needs the opposite: something dry and loose, like a sand-and-soil mix or aspen shavings, that will not trap dampness against the animal's belly.

Before you buy anything, look up three facts about your species: the humidity range it needs, whether it burrows, and whether it is likely to swallow bedding while feeding. Those three answers narrow the choice down to one or two good options.

## The main options, honestly assessed

**Coconut fibre** is the most versatile choice for tropical setups. It holds moisture well, resists mould, and is soft enough for burrowing species. It comes compressed in bricks that expand in water — a [coco fibre brick](/shop/substrate-decor/coco-fiber-brick-3-pack) is cheap and lasts a long time. The downside is that the surface dries out in a warm enclosure, so mist or pour water into the corners regularly.

**Cypress mulch** suits tropical and temperate species alike. It holds humidity without getting soggy, smells pleasantly woody, and is chunky enough that most reptiles will not swallow it by accident. [Cypress mulch](/shop/substrate-decor/cypress-mulch-24qt) is a good default for snakes like ball pythons and corn snakes. Avoid pine and cedar mulch entirely — both release aromatic oils that irritate reptile lungs.

**Aspen shavings** are the classic choice for arid snakes and some lizards. They are dry, cheap, easy to spot-clean and great for burrowing. They are a poor choice for anything that needs humidity above about 50%, because they mould quickly when damp.

**Topsoil and sand mixes** work well for desert lizards that dig, such as bearded dragons — provided the sand is fine, natural play sand mixed with plain topsoil, not dyed or calcium sand. Calcium sand clumps when wet and is a known impaction risk when swallowed.

**Paper towel and newspaper** are not glamorous, but they are the safest option for a new arrival in quarantine, a sick animal, or a hatchling. They are cheap, make it easy to see droppings and mites, and carry zero impaction risk. Many keepers use them for the first month and switch to a natural substrate once the animal is settled and eating.

## Substrates to avoid

Some products sold for reptiles are best left on the shelf. Calci-sand and other "digestible" sands are marketed as safe to swallow, but they clump in a damp gut and cause blockages. Walnut shell is sharp and dusty. Reptile carpet seems practical, but it traps bacteria, snags claws and teeth, and is hard to clean properly. Loose, dusty wood shavings from unknown sources can carry mould or chemical treatments. If a substrate's label does not say exactly what it is made of, do not use it.

## Depth, layering and maintenance

Most substrates work best at 5 to 8 cm deep — enough for burrowing species to dig, and enough material to buffer humidity. Our [reptile humidity guide](/blog/reptile-humidity-guide) explains how to measure and hold the right level once the substrate is in place. For a planted or bioactive setup, layer it: a drainage layer of clay balls at the bottom, mesh, then soil on top. Our guide to [setting up a bioactive terrarium](/blog/how-to-set-up-a-bioactive-terrarium) walks through that build step by step.

Spot-clean every day, removing droppings and any wet or soiled patches as soon as you see them. Do a full change every four to six weeks for most substrates, sooner if it starts to smell or you see mould. Never just top up old bedding over dirty bedding — the waste underneath keeps rotting and the bacteria count climbs.

## When to change your mind

If the enclosure smells musty, if humidity will not stay where it should, or if your animal keeps getting substrate stuck in its mouth or eyes, the bedding is wrong for that setup. Small adjustments often fix it: a deeper layer, a different mix — [sphagnum moss](/shop/substrate-decor/sphagnum-moss-long-fiber) helps hold humidity, for example — or a humid hide added on the side.

## Frequently asked questions

### How deep should substrate be?

Five to eight centimetres suits most species — deep enough to burrow in and to hold moisture. Burrowing species like sand boas or hognose snakes appreciate 10 cm or more.

### Can I mix substrates?

Yes, and it often works better than a single type. Coconut fibre mixed with cypress mulch, or topsoil mixed with play sand, combines the strengths of both. Just make sure every component is safe on its own first.

### How often should I replace substrate completely?

Every four to six weeks for most loose substrates, or immediately if you see mould, mites or a strong smell. Spot-clean daily in between.
