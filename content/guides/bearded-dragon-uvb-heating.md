Bearded dragons (*Pogona vitticeps*) are desert lizards that spend their days basking in intense Australian sunshine. Most health problems in captive dragons trace back to **weak UVB** or a **basking spot that isn't hot enough**. Here's how to set both up properly.

## Why UVB matters

UVB light lets a dragon's skin make vitamin D3, which it needs to absorb calcium. Without it, dragons develop **metabolic bone disease (MBD)**: soft bones, tremors and deformities. Supplements alone can't fully replace good UVB.

## Choosing a UVB light

- Use a **T5 HO 12% (desert) tube**. Compact coil bulbs and weak T8 tubes don't provide enough UV for a dragon.
- The tube should cover **one-half to two-thirds** of the enclosure length, for example a 34 in tube in a 4 ft enclosure.
- Mount it **inside the enclosure** or over a fine mesh, with a reflector. A screen top blocks a large share of the UV.
- The dragon's back should be about **12–15 in (30–38 cm)** from the tube while basking.
- **Replace the tube every 12 months.** UV output fades long before the visible light does.

## Heat and the basking spot

| Zone | Temperature |
| --- | --- |
| Basking surface | 100–110°F (38–43°C) for adults |
| Warm air | 85–90°F (29–32°C) |
| Cool side | 75–85°F (24–29°C) |
| Night | Down to 65°F (18°C), no light |

- Use **halogen flood bulbs**. Two smaller bulbs side by side warm the dragon's whole body instead of one small hot spot.
- Measure the basking surface with an **infrared thermometer**, not a stick-on dial.
- Control the bulbs with a **dimming thermostat** so the light stays steady.

## Daily schedule

Run UVB and basking lights together for **12–14 hours a day** on a timer, and turn all lights off at night. Dragons need darkness to sleep, so avoid colored "night bulbs".

## Common mistakes

1. A UVB light sitting on top of a fine screen that blocks most of the UV.
2. A basking spot that's warm in the air but cool on the surface.
3. UVB tubes older than a year.
4. Coil bulbs instead of linear tubes.
